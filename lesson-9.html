LESSON 9 / 11

Risk Management

Risk per trade, position sizing, risk/reward, worked examples and risk controls.

Study Sections

1. Risk per trade

Risk per trade is the amount you plan to lose if the trade idea fails, not the amount you hope to earn. A common educational framework sets a risk budget as a small fraction of account value, but there is no universally suitable percentage. Actual losses can exceed the plan because of gaps, slippage, illiquidity or execution failure. Define the risk limit before entry and consider whether a loss would affect essential expenses.

2. Position sizing

Position sizing links the planned risk budget to the distance between entry and the invalidation/stop level. In a simplified example, units equal risk budget divided by per-unit loss at the stop. Real instruments may use contract multipliers, tick values, lot sizes, currency conversion, margin, fees and slippage, so the simple formula may not apply directly. Always check the instrument’s specifications and never use a calculator result without understanding its assumptions.

3. Risk/reward

Risk/reward compares the distance to a possible target with the distance to the planned stop. A larger reward-to-risk ratio does not automatically mean a trade is better because the chance of reaching the target may also be lower. The ratio alone says nothing about win rate, costs or execution quality. Evaluate it as one planning input, not a prediction of profitability.

4. Worked examples

A worked example should show the account value, chosen risk budget, entry, stop, target, instrument units and assumptions. Calculate the potential loss first, then the potential gain, and state whether fees, spread, slippage and contract multipliers are included. Use hypothetical numbers to practice arithmetic rather than to suggest a live position. If assumptions are missing, label the result illustrative rather than exact.

5. Risk controls

Risk controls include predefined exposure limits, avoiding excessive leverage, accounting for correlated positions, and stopping when a rule or loss limit is reached. A stop order is not a guarantee of execution at its exact price, especially in fast or illiquid markets. Never increase size just to recover a previous loss. Review whether the process was followed, regardless of whether the trade won or lost.

Lesson Summary & Key Takeaways

Risk management starts by defining the amount that could be lost if an idea fails, not by estimating how much might be earned. In a simplified cash example, risk budget equals account value multiplied by the chosen risk fraction; illustrative units equal risk budget divided by the per-unit distance between entry and stop. Real instruments require contract specifications, tick values, currency conversion, fees, slippage and leverage adjustments, so this simple formula may not be sufficient. Risk/reward compares potential loss distance with target distance but does not establish probability of success. Stops can slip or fail to execute at the expected price. Avoid risking money you cannot afford to lose.
